Installation sketches

The fan can sit away from the grille.

The REC54 does not have to be directly above the bathroom inlet. An offset fan with a short, protected duct across the roof is worth evaluating for sound control. Both routes keep the duct above the wood ceiling.

02

Offset route: separate the fan from the shower inlet

Why offsetting may help

Separation can reduce the direct sound path and provide space for an appropriate duct silencer, if needed. An actual duct run of at least 4 feet can satisfy the length condition for the remote-fan sound exception, subject to its other conditions. This is duct length, not necessarily horizontal separation.

What the longer route adds

More duct and bends add resistance. Above-roof ductwork needs weather protection, insulation, supports, sealed joints and a condensation strategy. A low weatherproof chase is one option; ordinary attic flex duct left exposed on the roof is not the proposed detail.

Basis: Fantech manual, pages 3–4; 2025 CEC ventilation form, page 6; PNNL duct-design guidance. The finished assembly must deliver the required airflow; distance alone does not establish quietness or full compliance.

Direct route: the shortest connection

Locating the curb above the grille reduces duct length and rooftop equipment footprint. It also leaves a shorter path for fan sound to reach the room, and may not meet the length condition for the remote-fan sound exception.

Alternative · Fan inside the bathroom

A shallow fan in a local soffit.

The Panasonic FV-0510VS1 saves four inches of fan-body depth. A finished enclosure below the tongue-and-groove could house it without placing the motor in a rooftop enclosure. Proposed enclosure

A narrow, finished strip aligned with a beam or wall.

Mount the fan in its normal ceiling orientation using its specified brackets on supported framing. Put its original grille in the soffit’s underside. Extend the enclosure along the side outlet to conceal the duct before it turns toward a flashed roof termination.

Use bathroom-appropriate, wipeable finishes; a painted surface or sealed wood finish can be compared with the existing ceiling. Provide removable, gasketed inspection access beside the fan for the duct and electrical connections. Keep the grille removable and preserve required service access.

This is a custom construction proposal, not a Panasonic enclosure kit. Keep the soffit on the indoor side of the roof’s insulation and air barrier. Confirm mounting, finish materials, headroom, tub/shower GFCI protection and the full duct route before fabrication.

Planning dimensions for a contractor mock-up

These are preliminary design allowances derived from the fan and duct geometry, not manufacturer-required enclosure sizes or a confirmed fit. A cardboard mock-up should use the contractor’s completed dimensions.

48–60″ long

6–8″ finished drop

Initial allowance including a 4″ round duct, finish and projecting grille. The elbow, insulation and headroom may change it; 3⅜″ is only the fan-body depth. The body-depth saving does not guarantee a four-inch reduction in the finished soffit.

Two matching grilles on one elongated enclosure?

Yes, as a custom appearance and access detail. Keep the original grille under the fan, then use an identical replacement grille beneath the duct compartment. Both grilles are sold separately; choose the part for the exact fan model.

Both fans specify a nominal 13″ square grille, but the parts are different. Encompass lists the VS1 grille as out of stock when checked; confirm availability before ordering. Factory grille springs normally attach to slots in the fan housing. A second grille over the duct compartment needs its own supported mounting detail and must remain removable for access.

The second grille would not be a second exhaust inlet.

Fan grille → fan → sealed duct → outdoors.
Extra open grille → cavity around the duct.

With the fan flange sealed to the underside in the usual way, fan suction does not intentionally pass through the surrounding cavity. An open extra grille allows passive exchange with bathroom air; it does not establish forced drying of that cavity. Creating a second active inlet would need a deliberately designed, manufacturer-reviewed intake arrangement. Do not add openings in the fan casing or exhaust duct.

For matching appearance with the closed-soffit proposal, the spare grille could instead be attached to a gasketed, removable solid access panel. It would then be decorative, with no ventilation function. An open grille is a different moisture-control choice for the designer to assess below.

Would vents help the enclosure dry?

Openings could help an entirely indoor cavity exchange air as the bathroom dries, but they also admit humid shower air. Passive slots do not establish a reliable drying airflow; colder roof or duct surfaces can still collect condensation. That is a building-physics concern, not a Panasonic prohibition on decorative vents.

Proposed starting detail: a closed soffit with inspection access, without added side vents. Seal the fan flange to the finished underside and mechanically connect and seal the duct. Maintain the roof’s insulation and air barrier, seal penetrations, and insulate cold duct sections. Close the assembly only when its materials are dry. Use an appropriate timer or humidity control and verify installed airflow.

Keep the exhaust entirely inside the fan and duct. The surrounding box is not an exhaust plenum. Do not obstruct the original grille or manufacturer-required openings. If a vented surround is preferred, have the designer check cavity temperature, airflow and drying; its vents must not open into the roof assembly. Neither vent slots nor moisture-resistant finishes substitute for correcting leaks.

Sources: Panasonic manual, pages 3–8; PNNL installation and duct-sealing guidance; EPA moisture and mold guidance. The soffit dimensions and choice of an unvented surround are project proposals inferred from these principles, not a published manufacturer detail.

Contractor discussion brief

Ten questions before ordering.

Eichler bathroom · No attic · Tongue-and-groove ceiling directly below roof · FV-0511VQ1 already installed. Request coordinated roof-fan and Panasonic-soffit proposals from the roofer and ventilation/electrical installer.

05

What is actually above the ceiling?

Record opening width/length, board thickness, roof layers, clear depth, roof material/slope, and beam locations. Confirm support for cut boards.

Which complete layout fits?

Compare the direct/offset REC54 with the FV-0510VS1 soffit. Show finished dimensions, mounting, headroom, the side-duct straight run and bend, roof termination, drainage and warranty treatment. Confirm custom details with the relevant manufacturer.

How will the grille cover and attach?

For Fantech, show the frame, transition and removable grille. For Panasonic, retain its original grille in the finished soffit underside; show separate support and access for any matching spare grille over the duct compartment. Show repair/support of the existing opening if the new duct route does not reuse it.

How will the concealed space stay dry?

Detail sealed fan flange and duct joints, roof air sealing, insulation, condensation control and backdraft protection. For the soffit, show inspection access and dry materials before closure. Any proposed side vents need an explicit airflow and drying assessment.

What airflow will the room receive?

Establish required exhaust from bathroom size and operating mode. Calculate resistance through the grille, transition, duct and damper; measure completed airflow.

What is the sound-compliance route?

For Fantech, state grille-to-fan duct length, expected sound and any silencer; confirm the 4-foot condition if using the remote-fan exception. For Panasonic, assess rated sound at the proposed airflow and duct resistance.

Is the exact unit currently certified?

Obtain current model/item-specific ENERGY STAR evidence and confirm the permit’s ventilation requirements with the city or county authority.

Which controls and electrical details?

Specify humidity control, user operation, wiring route, and service access. Confirm the manufacturer’s tub/shower and GFCI requirements for this arrangement.

How will it be cleaned and serviced?

Confirm removable grille, accessible damper/transition as needed, roof-fan access, and vibration isolation without exposing the roof to leaks.

What does the complete bid include?

Itemize fan, grille/frame, duct/transition, damper, controls, wiring, curb/flashing, insulation, roof repair, permits and commissioning. Identify who owns each detail.

Why current certification still needs checking

The saved 2007 Fantech sheet and January 2009 EPA list show historical ENERGY STAR qualification. They do not establish certification for the unit that would ship today. The 2025 CALGreen checklist, §4.506.1, addresses outdoor exhaust, ENERGY STAR fans and humidity control. Permit scope, code edition and local applicability need confirmation.

Why a roof fan is not automatically a quiet bathroom fan

The 2025 CEC ventilation form, page 6, gives local exhaust sound limits and a remote-fan exception with location conditions and at least 4 feet of duct between fan and grille. The Fantech’s historical AMCA free-field fan-sones values are not directly comparable with HVI bathroom ratings. An outdoor motor can still transmit sound through a short duct.

What remains open in the design

Actual roof construction, opening size, bathroom size, fan certification, installed sound, grille choice, controls and the final roof detail remain open. This page does not change the project’s product decisions. The full research report includes the Panasonic and Broan alternatives.
