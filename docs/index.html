Design direction

A convincing Eichler bath uses a few era-appropriate moves with modern performance — it doesn't need to look like a period set.

  • Warm wood + one saturated tile color. A simple floating walnut or teak vanity paired with a single expressive tile color — forest green and mid-century blue are the strongest — carries most of the character.
  • Calm envelope. Keep the remaining surfaces white or warm-neutral so the wood, color, and natural light do the work.
  • Open, glazed wet zone. Frameless glass, a walk-in shower, a tiled bench, and (where possible) a tub near the glazing for a garden view.
  • Respect the small footprint. A better vanity, storage, lighting, and glass can transform a compact original plan without chasing a larger suite.
  • Quiet fixtures & lighting. Chrome/nickel or aged brass, low-profile faucets, a large frameless mirror, and globe sconces or pendants; lean on the skylight for daytime light.

Shower floor & tile — the terrazzo look, done safely

Slip standard: U.S. ANSI A326.3 sets a minimum DCOF ≥ 0.42 for interior level wet floors — treat that as the floor, not the goal. For the shower pan, use small mosaics (2×2, penny, hex): more grout lines add traction and small tiles follow the slope to the drain. On European tile you may see R11–R12 or barefoot-wet P4–P5 / B–C for showers.

Pan floor — mosaic, slip-appropriate

  • Unglazed porcelain speckle mosaic (e.g. Daltile Keystones 2×2) — classic slip-safe shower floor; speckle reads terrazzo-ish; small format handles the slope.
  • Mosaic version of a terrazzo-look porcelain — ask for the 2×2 / penny / hex sheet of a terrazzo collection; confirm matte (not polished) and DCOF ≥ 0.42; use sanded grout.

Walls + bath floor — large-format terrazzo look

  • Daltile Outlander (StepWise) — colorbody terrazzo look, DCOF ≥ 0.42 wet. Great on walls/bath floor; not the pan (large format only).
  • Marazzi D_Segni Terrazzo — crisp classic-terrazzo scatter, very mid-century.
  • Also sample: MSI Arterra Terrazo, Daltile Sand & Stone, American Olean Color Story.

Using real terrazzo tile in the pan — yes, with conditions

  • Finish: honed/matte or lightly textured — polished terrazzo is slippery wet.
  • Format: small (mosaic/small squares) so it sits on the pan's slope; keep large terrazzo tiles for walls/curb/bench.
  • Sealing: cement terrazzo is porous — seal at install and reseal ~1–2×/yr; the pan still needs its own waterproof membrane.
  • Common compromise: real terrazzo on walls/curb/bench + a slip-rated small mosaic on the floor.

Sources for slip/tile guidance: CTASC (ANSI A326.3), Daltile StepWise, Apollo Tile, DM Terrazzo.

Eichler-specific notes for bidding

⚠ Asbestos — read before any demolition

This is a 1964 house. Assume any original 9″×9″ floor tile — and the black mastic under it — contains asbestos until laboratory testing says otherwise. Original Eichlers were floored in vinyl-asbestos tile (VAT); the adhesive is frequently the greater hazard. Newer 12″×12″ vinyl tile is usually fine, but age is not a reliable guide and only a lab test settles it.

Intact tile underfoot is not dangerous. It becomes dangerous when it is broken, sanded, scraped, drilled, or dry-swept — which is exactly what demolition does. Do not disturb suspect flooring before it has been sampled.

Required approach: test first via a certified lab; then either encapsulate (leave in place and float the new floor over it — usually cheaper, and permitted when the material stays intact) or remove it. In California, asbestos work over 100 sq ft at >0.1% concentration must be performed by a Cal/OSHA-registered asbestos contractor, with air-district notification and hazardous-waste disposal. Bidders should price testing as a separate line item and state which path they assume.

  • Slab & radiant heat: slab-on-grade with (often failed) radiant copper. Map and test radiant, water, and sewer before demo; electric heating mats are the common replacement.
  • Drain relocation is a major cost — moving drains/supply can mean opening the slab near radiant lines. Favor a pan whose drain matches existing plumbing.
  • Curbless? A barrier-free pan needs the slab recessed — decide early; it drives demo and framing.
  • Structure: post-and-beam framing limits attic/crawl access for new ducts/wiring; plan runs accordingly.
  • Preserve useful geometry: a better vanity, storage, lighting, and glass can transform the existing footprint without expanding it.
  • These references are design inspiration and product leads — confirm waterproofing, ventilation, tempered-glass, electrical, and code requirements as part of the bid.
