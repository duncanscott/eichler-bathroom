# Terrazzo Flooring for the Bathroom Floor

Researched August 9, 2026. Companion to `custom-terrazzo-shower-bases.md` — this covers the **floor outside the shower**, chosen to be durable, low-slip, and to sit well with the cast terrazzo pan.

## The one spec that decides slip: grind level, not material

This is the most useful thing to know about terrazzo, and it's not obvious. Terrazzo's slipperiness is set almost entirely by **how far it's ground and what sealer goes on it**, not by the aggregate or the binder:

- **Lower grit = more slip-resistant.** Higher grit produces the mirror sheen everyone associates with terrazzo — and that sheen *is* the slipperiness.
- **Sealer matters as much as grind.** For a bathroom, specify a water-based sealer explicitly rated **"anti-slip when wet,"** not a general-purpose gloss sealer. A polished floor with the wrong sealer will be dangerous no matter what it's made of.

So the instruction to the fabricator is: **honed/matte finish, low grit, anti-slip-rated water-based sealer, tested wet DCOF ≥ 0.42 (ANSI A326.3)** for the exact finish you're buying. Ask for the test result for that finish, not for the product line.

Do not accept a polished terrazzo floor in a bathroom.

## Recommended: precast epoxy terrazzo tile from the same fabricator as the pan

The strongest option is precast **epoxy** terrazzo tile ordered from whoever casts the shower base, so the aggregate, background color, and chip density match rather than nearly-match.

**Angelozzi precast terrazzo tile** — https://angelozziterrazzo.com/precast-terrazzo-tile/ · (856) 753-3200

- Standard sizes **12×12, 24×24, 30×30, 24×48**; custom sizes and shapes on request.
- **Epoxy tiles: 3/8″ standard**, and can go as thin as **1/4″** if the aggregate allows — genuinely useful here, because a thin floor keeps door clearances and the transition to adjacent flooring manageable, and thin tile over a slab transmits radiant heat better.
- Cement-based tiles are also offered but are **1/2″ or thicker with lower psi** — heavier, thicker, more porous. For a bathroom, epoxy is the better pick for the same reasons it wins for the pan.
- They make shower units, tile, wall panels, and slabs from the same Terralite material, so a matched floor/pan/curb/bench set is plausible — **confirm matching explicitly when you call**, since their tile page doesn't state it.

Why epoxy for the floor as well: non-porous, so no sealing regime and no staining from the inevitable splashes; lighter and thinner; more crack-resistant than cement terrazzo over a slab that may move.

## Format and layout for a small bathroom

- **Large format (24×24 or 24×48) reads best** — fewer grout lines means the floor reads as a continuous plane, which is the whole point of choosing terrazzo over patterned tile, and it echoes the monolithic cast pan. In a compact Eichler bath this makes the room feel larger.
- **But grout lines add traction**, so if the floor runs right up to a curbless shower entry where water regularly lands, consider dropping to 12×12 in that zone, or rely on the low-grit finish and a good sealer instead.
- Keep the **floor and pan flush** at a curbless entry — this is a fabrication question to settle up front, since floor tile thickness plus setting bed has to equal the pan's edge height.

## How to relate the floor to the pan

Two defensible approaches:

1. **Continuous — same material, same aggregate, floor and pan read as one surface.** Most architectural, most Eichler, and the reason to buy both from one fabricator. Best if the aggregate is restrained.
2. **Quiet floor, expressive pan** — a near-solid or very sparsely flecked floor with slightly more aggregate character in the shower. Safer if you like a busier terrazzo but don't want the whole room speckled.

Given the aesthetic already agreed — warm light base (off-white or pale warm gray) with **sparse** charcoal, rust, muted green, and cream aggregate — approach 1 is the recommendation. That palette is quiet enough to carry across the entire floor without fighting a saturated green or blue tile wall, and it stays clear of the busy "confetti terrazzo" look.

## Lower-maintenance alternative: terrazzo-look porcelain

If custom cast tile proves slow or expensive, **terrazzo-look porcelain** gets most of the way there for the floor (not the pan, where the cast base is the point):

- **Daltile Outlander with StepWise** — colorbody terrazzo look, published **DCOF ≥ 0.42 wet**, sizes 12×24 / 24×24 / 24×48. Rated for wet interior floors and walls. https://www.daltile.com/products/terrazzo-look/outlander/dusk-rectangle-12x24-alto-stepwise-matte
- **Marazzi D_Segni Terrazzo** — crisper, more classic mid-century scatter.

Trade-off: zero maintenance and a known slip number, but it's a print rather than aggregate, and it won't match a cast pan exactly — which pushes you toward the "quiet floor, expressive pan" approach rather than a continuous one.

## Checklist for the floor order

1. Finish: **honed/matte, low grit** — get the grind level in writing.
2. Sealer: water-based, **"anti-slip when wet."**
3. **Tested wet DCOF ≥ 0.42** for that exact finish and sealer combination.
4. Thickness: confirm total build-up (tile + setting bed) against door clearances, the shower pan edge, and transitions to adjacent rooms.
5. Confirm the floor aggregate and background **match the shower base** if going continuous — ideally from the same pour/batch.
6. Radiant-slab note: thin tile set directly on the slab conducts heat well; avoid thick mortar beds that insulate.
