# What owners and designers say about covering an Eichler fireplace

Recorded September 10, 2026. [Return to the illustrated report](README.md) · [Project chat reconstruction](discussion.md) · [Image ledger](image-sources.csv)

This file re-creates the public conversations that bear on tiling over an Eichler fireplace. Quotations are taken verbatim from the linked pages and attributed to the screen name or author used there. Where a passage is condensed, it is marked as paraphrase. Forum posts on Eichler Network are from the CA-Modern "Home Maintenance Hotline" forum and are old (2004 to 2008); products and contacts mentioned there have not been checked.

## 1. "Modern fireplace suggestions?" — Eichler Network forum, May 2007

Source: [eichlernetwork.com/content/modern-fireplace-suggestions](https://www.eichlernetwork.com/content/modern-fireplace-suggestions)

**oldskoolboarder** (new owner, May 18, 2007), opening the thread:

> "We're painting the house before we move in and our designer wants to bring in colors from the garden into the house, so that there's more continuity. She doesn't like the fireplace (neither do I) and was thinking of using granite tiles to match the color of a backyard tree that has dark red leaves … I would prefer to the keep the MCM aesthetic but still want to have something dark like that, preferably not granite. I'd be OK w/ brick, something painted, or concrete but thought I'd come here for ideas. There may be brick underneath the granite but that might be a pain to resurrect."

**DavidHatt** (May 19, 2007):

> "Regarding your fireplace, I had my fireplace Venetian glazed with a salmon color put in the pigment. It took the faux finisher several layers of plaster because of the brick work, and the results were excellent. With the Venetian plaster, you can match the pigment to anything you want."

**tborsellino** (May 19, 2007), pushing back and listing modernist options:

> "Salmon colored Venetian plaster is meant to mimic something that looks several hundred years old and doesn't really address his inquiry for a mid-century modern solution. … Some possibilities: Check on any books on Richard Neutra homes. He typically used flagstone laid horizontally. You can get veneers that give the same look and attach right over your current fireplace. This would be a good way to bring the outside in. Raw, grey concrete is another totally modern choice. It would probably look even better polished. Black slate or soapstone tiles. Cover it in one-inch glass mosaic tiles. Stainless steel. The 1950's era Sunset book 'How to Plan and Build Your Fireplace' even has one clad in corrugated aluminum panels. Ceramic subway tiles installed vertically. It looks like you have cinderblock, which looks way better than the clinker brick Eichler fireplaces. You could try to strip it. … Contrasting colors that play up the geometry of your home will likely work better (like black beams against the ceiling). Your bigger challenge may be figuring out to blend the internal look of the fireplace with the external since you have windows on either side of the hearth."

**cstetter** (May 19, 2007) offered a concrete-countertop fabricator who lives in an Eichler (contact details omitted).

*Takeaway:* the community's instinct is toward materials that read modern and geometric (slate, soapstone, glass mosaic, vertical subway, concrete, stone veneer) rather than granite or plaster that imitates age, and toward keeping the inside and outside faces of a glass-flanked chimney consistent.

## 2. "'UN'-painting a fireplace" — Eichler Network forum, April 2005

Source: [eichlernetwork.com/content/un-painting-fireplace](https://www.eichlernetwork.com/content/un-painting-fireplace)

**geminder** asked how to get painted interior brick back to original. Most replies (Tod, Ben, jxbrown, Leslie, Randy at Dura-Foam) debated paint strippers, Peel Away, torching and sand-blasting, and warned that blasting erodes soft brick and is hard to contain in an occupied house. Randy's view (paraphrase): true restoration is not really possible because paint soaks into porous brick and blasting rounds the corners and changes the texture.

**Harris** (April 20, 2005) then gave the tile-over answer:

> "Another solution is to skip the sandblasting and resurface with stone that has an Eichler 'feel.' Our painted fireplace presented the same challenge, and we couldn't face the mess presented by sandblasting. So we resurfaced the fireplace with some lovely natural stone tiles that have a contemporary look. Unless you are a real Eichler purist, I think you would find this solution very satisfactory."

*Takeaway:* covering damaged or painted brick with tile is an accepted, if less purist, path in the owner community. The same thread is a good summary of why stripping is hard.

## 3. "Mason Recommendations for Chimney Facelift" — Eichler Network forum, July to October 2004

Source: [eichlernetwork.com/content/mason-recommendations-chimney-facelift](https://www.eichlernetwork.com/content/mason-recommendations-chimney-facelift)

(The picture is no longer on the page.)

*Takeaway:* by 2004 owners were already refacing painted Eichler chimneys with slate, stone veneer and smooth stucco. Slate and a smooth plaster plane are both period-plausible.

## 4. "Re-finish Cinder Block Fireplace" — Eichler Network forum, February 2008

Source: [eichlernetwork.com/content/re-finish-cinder-block-fireplace](https://www.eichlernetwork.com/content/re-finish-cinder-block-fireplace)

**mryguy** (Portland, Oregon, Jones-designed house) wanted to add "slate or some other green toned tile/stone to the cinder block surface" to hide water stains weeping through paint, and worried about heat from a gas insert on a glued application.

**bws** (February 28, 2008):

> "you can easily tile on top of your blocks, but you'll use mortar not glue. go to a tile shop or big box store and they'll give you the correct stuff - and it's cheap. sounds like you've got to solve your water issue first, tho. any natural stone/tile you put on top might also absorb stains. ceramic & porcelain do not absorb stains, but you'll want to use a natural stone. ceramic on the floor is one thing, but ceramic covering a fireplace could look cheesy - IMHO. slate would look great."

**tborsellino** added that the water was probably entering at the top of the chimney and should be sealed there first; mryguy replied that four sealant attempts had failed and asked whether changing the original fireplace would hurt resale.

*Takeaway:* two practical rules from this thread. Fix the cause of any staining or moisture before covering it, because tile will not stop it and porous stone will absorb it. Set tile in mortar or thin-set, not adhesive, around a heat source.

## 5. "Removal of Fireplace Hearth" — Eichler Network forum, January to February 2006

Source: [eichlernetwork.com/content/removal-fireplace-hearth](https://www.eichlernetwork.com/content/removal-fireplace-hearth)

The thread is mostly about removing the raised hearth (Jake and Ben raised the code and resale questions; bbrisco noted a non-combustible floor makes the hearth unnecessary). Two posts bear on tile:

**cathye** (February 2, 2006):

> "When we had our entire house tiled several years ago, we had them cover the hearth with the same tiles. The brick had already been painted flat Navaho White, and our tiles are 13x13 light beige with very subtle veining. IMHO, we love the look as from a distance it looks like one continuous floor."

**Dana** (February 10, 2006):

> "Our fireplace was painted over before we bought the house and after all of these years even with a better color of paint on it, it's still looking old and outdated now. I know the 'proper' way of redoing your fireplace in Eichlers is whatever you do on the inside you should do on the outside but the fireplace is just looking old now and would bring down the look we want in the living room."

*Takeaway:* the inside/outside rule again, and a reminder that the hearth can be tiled to match the floor.

## 6. John Klopf on an owner who insisted on tiling — Truly Open Eichler Home, Palo Alto, 2015

Source: [homedsgn.com — Truly Open Eichler Home by Klopf Architecture](https://www.homedsgn.com/2015/03/30/truly-open-eichler-home-by-klopf-architecture-arterra-landscape-architects-flegels-construction/) (also on [Dexigner](https://www.dexigner.com/news/27941))

> "Our original design called for removing it, but the owners really wanted to keep a fireplace. Sometimes Eichler fireplaces are pretty massive, and often they're right in the way of the view. That was definitely the case in this home, but as adamant as we were that it should go, they wanted to put nice tiles on the fireplace and keep it anyway. It was one of those situations in a design project where both sides understand the other, still disagree, and in the end the owner gets their way."

The published photographs of that house do not show the tiled fireplace clearly, so no image from this project is included.

*Takeaway:* an architect who works on Eichlers constantly treats "put nice tiles on it and keep it" as a legitimate owner choice, even where the architect would rather remove the mass.

## 7. Karen Nepacena (Destination Eichler) on finding tile over her own brick — Walnut Creek, 1959 A. Quincy Jones Eichler

Sources: [Stripping Down: Restoring a Fireplace](https://www.destinationeichler.com/blog/restoring-a-brick-fireplace) · [Dining Room and Fireplace Reveal](https://www.destinationeichler.com/blog/dining-room-and-fireplace-reveal) · [Atomic Ranch profile](https://www.atomic-ranch.com/architecture-design/eichler-renovation-walnut-creek/)

> "The original brick fireplace was tiled over with some beige stone that didn't fit the MCM theme."

> "Fireplace after tile removal. There was a thin layer of who-knows-what that was applied to the brick. It was much more difficult to remove than regular paint. We tried paint remover, but it didn't make a dent. I then tried soda blasting with a Harbor Freight 15lb blaster with a rented 6.5cfm @ 90 psi compressor, and that resulted in removing about a course of brick every two hours - ugh!"

> "It was time to bring in the professionals … A used professional abrasive blasting rig ran about $15K, so instead we hired Steve Coe out of Wheatland, CA. … They initially tested pure baking soda that didn't quite have the abrasion they wanted, so they also mixed in glass bead. It was loud and dusty, even with all the dust control mechanisms (a sealed area and a vacuum that pulled air out to the driveway). They were done in 4 hours and it was worth every penny!"

The reveal post opens: "After a few very messy weeks (think smashed travertine tile, two failed soda blasting attempts, and living without glass panels …)". Atomic Ranch summarised the same work: "They even knocked travertine off the fireplace and laboriously restored the original brick underneath."

*Takeaway:* the most complete documented case of tile over Eichler brick, told from the point of view of an owner who removed it. Two lessons for anyone tiling now: the setting bed bonds into the brick face and is far harder to remove than paint, so tiling is close to a one-way decision, and a flat stone veneer is what an Eichler specialist judged "didn't fit the MCM theme". The same designer later re-clad fireplaces in glazed thin brick and handpainted tile for clients (images 08, 09, 12 to 15), which suggests the objection was to the material, not to tiling as such.

## 10. Eichler For Sale on preserving brick — September 2025

Source: [Eichler Fireplace Design and History](https://www.eichlerforsale.com/blog/eichler-fireplace-design-and-history/), Renee Adelmann

> "Eichler fireplaces were built in several different materials. Some were formed in simple red fire brick, others in rugged cinder block, and every once in a while you'll come across a rare light-toned cream brick that feels like a true design gem."

> "One tip we always share with our clients is to think carefully before painting fireplace brick. The natural patina, texture, and variation in Eichler fireplaces are part of their DNA. Whenever possible, restoration or cleaning is a better approach than paint, which can cover up decades of character that make these fireplaces special."

*Takeaway:* the preservation and resale view. It argues against painting rather than against tiling, but the logic is the same.

## 11. fogmodern on stripping a painted brick fireplace — February 2017

Source: [eichler fireplace restoration](https://fogmodern.com/eichler-fireplace-restoration/)

Owners Ivy and Kyle (San Jose, Fairhaven) stripped eight coats of paint by hand: "I chose a brick on the side near the wall and scraped off some paint to see what we would be dealing with. … there were four paint colors, so probably at least eight coats." They used Ready Strip, Peel Away, wire brushes and finally an orbital sander with 60-grit paper, noting the sander removed "a little of the brick with it". The blog's verdict: "Most folks (us included) presumed painted Eichler fireplaces couldn't be saved – Ivy and Kyle just proved us wrong."

*Takeaway:* the DIY counter-argument to tiling. It shows what "damage" often is (paint) and the labour needed to reverse it.

## Reading the conversation as a whole

- Tiling over Eichler brick is common enough that owners, an Eichler architect and an Eichler-specialist designer all treat it as a real option, and thin brick or slate are the materials with the most support.
- The recurring objection is to the wrong material (travertine, beige stone, faux-old plaster), not to tile itself.
- Whatever goes on must be set in mortar or thin-set, and it will bond into the brick; removal later means abrasive blasting.
- Fix moisture or staining first; tile hides it but does not stop it.
- Where a chimney passes through glass, owners repeatedly say the inside and outside faces should read as one material.
